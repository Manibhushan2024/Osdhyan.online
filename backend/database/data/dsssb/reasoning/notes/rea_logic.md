# Syllogism, Statements, Venn Diagrams & Mathematical Operations

**In 30 seconds**
- This is DSSSB's biggest reasoning block: **24 of 61** PYQs ⭐. Mathematical operations alone gave 8 (sign replacement 4, sign interchange 2, symbol substitution 1, number interchange 1).
- Sign replacement ⭐: find the pair that divides exactly (221 ÷ 17, 69 ÷ 23, 42 ÷ 21), test only the options that put "÷" there, and apply **BODMAS** — ÷ and × before + and −, left to right.
- Statement & conclusion ⭐: a conclusion follows only if the statement itself proves it. Reasons guessed for an event, new facts and extreme words (all, only, always, every) do not follow.
- Course of action ⭐: it must be practical, aimed at the problem, and proportionate. "Punish all employees", "leave the town", "ban for every citizen" never follow.
- Syllogism ⭐: draw the smallest possible Venn picture; a conclusion follows only if it is true in **every** picture. Some + Some gives nothing; All + All gives All.
- Coded inequalities ⭐: decode the symbols, write one chain, and combine only signs that point the same way (> with ≥ gives >).
- Venn diagrams: subset = circle inside circle; overlap = some belong to both; separate = nothing common. Numbers: n(A ∪ B) = n(A) + n(B) − n(A ∩ B).
- Approximation ⭐: round every number to the nearest whole number first, then BODMAS, then pick the closest option.

## 1. Syllogism ⭐

### The four statement types

| Type | Form | Venn picture in words | Converts to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Universal positive | All A are B | Circle A inside circle B | Some B are A |
| Universal negative | No A is B | A and B separate | No B is A |
| Particular positive | Some A are B | A and B overlap | Some B are A |
| Particular negative | Some A are not B | Part of A outside B | No conversion |

### Combining two statements (middle term shared)

| First + second | Conclusion | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| All + All | All | All cats are dogs; all dogs are birds → **All cats are birds** (and Some birds are cats). |
| All + No | No | All pencils are pens; no pen is a rubber → **No pencil is a rubber**, No rubber is a pencil. |
| Some + All | Some | Some dogs are goats; all goats are peacocks → **Some dogs are peacocks** ⭐ → Some peacocks are dogs. |
| Some + No | Some … not | Some men are kings; no king is a thief → **Some men are not thieves**. |
| All + Some | No definite conclusion | All teachers are scholars; some scholars are doctors → "Some teachers are doctors" does **not** follow. |
| No + All | Some (reversed) … not | No flower is a stone; all stones are rocks → **Some rocks are not flowers**; "No flower is a rock" does not follow. |
| Some + Some | Nothing | Some tables are chairs; some chairs are fans → no relation between tables and fans. |
| No + No | Nothing | No A is B; no B is C → A and C may or may not meet. |

Order matters: the middle term must be the predicate of the first statement and the subject of the second for the table to work directly. If not, convert one statement first.

### Three-statement chains ⭐

| Method | Worked example |
|---|---|
| Combine statements 1 + 2, then the result + 3 | Some dogs are goats; all goats are peacocks; all peacocks are eagles. I. Some peacocks are dogs ✓. II. All dogs are peacocks ✗ (only some). III. All eagles are peacocks ✗ (reverse of "all peacocks are eagles"). → **Only I** ⭐. |
| If no pair gives a definite result, nothing follows | Some C are D; some D are E; no E is G. I. Some E are C ✗ (Some + Some). II. No D is G ✗ (the D outside E may be G). III. Some G are C ✗. → **Neither** ⭐. |

### Either–or (complementary pair)

Two conclusions about the **same two terms** where one must be true if the other is false: "Some A are B" / "No A is B", or "All A are B" / "Some A are not B". If neither follows on its own, answer "Either I or II follows". Worked example: some tables are chairs; some chairs are fans → I. Some tables are fans; II. No table is a fan → **either I or II**.

### "Possibility" conclusions
"All A being B is a possibility" follows if one valid picture shows it and no statement forbids it. A possibility never follows when a "No" statement blocks it.

## 2. Statement & conclusions ⭐

| Follows | Does not follow |
|---|---|
| Directly stated or a necessary result of the statement | A reason guessed for the event ("he worked harder") |
| A mild, likely result ("prices are likely to rise") | Extreme words: all, every, only, always, never, definitely |
| Restatement in other words | New information not in the statement (a name, a date, a disease) |
| Purpose clearly stated ("to reach the world market" → wants global market) | Value judgments about people or organisations |

Worked examples:
- ABC Corporation plans to expand to reach the world market ⭐ → "ABC wants to capture the global market" **follows**; "ABC is not competitive" does **not**.
- Raveena goes to the gym because her father advised her ⭐ → "Her father cares for her health" **follows**; "She has a chronic disease" is new information, does **not** follow.
- Ravi scored 95% this year against 60% last year → "His marks improved" follows; "He worked harder" does not (it guesses the cause).
- Only graduates can apply for TGT → "A non-graduate cannot apply" follows; "Every graduate will be selected" does not.

## 3. Course of action ⭐

| A course of action follows if it … | It does not follow if it … |
|---|---|
| Solves, reduces or prevents the problem | Is extreme or punishes everyone ("all employees") |
| Is practical and within the authority's power | Runs away from the problem ("leave the town") |
| Is proportionate to the problem | Is out of proportion ("ban mobiles for all citizens") |
| Checks or verifies facts before action | Is a general wish with no action |

Worked examples:
- Roads have deteriorated ⭐ → "Regular inspection and maintenance" **follows**; "Strict action against ALL employees" does **not** → **only II**.
- A bridge collapsed due to poor construction ⭐ → "Search-and-rescue at once" follows; "People should leave town Y" does not → **only I**.
- False dowry cases are increasing ⭐ → "Verify complaints before an FIR" and "act against false cases" both target the problem → **both** (official key).
- Dengue in a colony → fogging and removing stagnant water follows; asking people to leave permanently does not.

## 4. Statement & arguments

| Strong argument | Weak argument |
|---|---|
| Directly related to the question | Personal opinion or taste ("old-fashioned") |
| Gives a real, important reason or consequence | Trivial or narrow ("a tailor will lose business") |
| Applies generally, not to one person | Over-general ("everything should be free") |
| Backed by an established fact | Speculative ("may not have equipment") without support |

Worked examples:
- Ban plastic bags? Yes — they pollute and clog drains (**strong**). No — they are cheap (**weak**).
- Raise teachers' retirement age? Yes — eases the teacher shortage (strong). No — fewer jobs for young graduates (strong) → **both strong**.
- DSSSB 2023 ⭐ (college course without university affiliation): the official key marked **neither** strong — "more opportunities" is vague and "may not have resources" is a guess.

Assumption questions (related type): an assumption is something the speaker must take for granted. "Use brand X for whiter clothes" assumes people want whiter clothes and that the advertisement will be read; it does not assume other brands are bad.

## 5. Coded inequalities ⭐

| Combination along a chain | Result | Worked example |
|---|---|---|
| > and > | > | A > B > C → A > C. |
| > and ≥ (same direction) | > | A ≥ B > C → A > C. |
| ≥ and ≥ | ≥ | A ≥ B ≥ C → A ≥ C (so "A > C" alone does not follow). |
| = with any sign | Keeps that sign | A = B > C → A > C. |
| Opposite directions | No relation | A > B < C → nothing about A and C. |

Method: (1) translate every symbol, (2) write one chain, (3) read each conclusion along the chain.

Worked example ⭐: # = greater than, $ = greater than or equal to, = means smaller than. C $ E, F # C, D = E → C ≥ E, F > C, D < E → F > C ≥ E > D. I. C # D (C > D) ✓; II. E # D (E > D) ✓ → **both follow**.

Either–or here: if the chain gives A ≥ B, then "A > B" and "A = B" do not follow singly, but **either one** does.

## 6. Data sufficiency

| Answer pattern | When |
|---|---|
| I alone | I answers the question; II does not |
| II alone | II answers; I does not |
| Either alone | Each statement on its own answers it |
| Both together | Neither alone, but combined they do |
| Neither | Even together they fail |

Worked examples:
- Who is the shortest? ⭐ I: Sachin is taller than Mohan and shorter than Vipin. II: Devika is shorter than Gangan. The two chains never connect → **neither** (both insufficient).
- Code for HUMAN? ⭐ I: FAHAD = 61814; II: WATER = 23120518. Each shows letters replaced by alphabet positions → **either alone** is enough.
- Is X the tallest of X, Y, Z? I: X > Y. II: Y > Z. → **both together**.

## 7. Venn diagrams

### Word groups (describe the picture in words)

| Relationship | Picture | Example |
|---|---|---|
| One inside another, third separate | Small circle inside big circle; third circle apart | Surgeons inside Doctors; Teachers separate. |
| Two separate circles inside a big one | Two non-touching circles in one large circle | Pen and Pencil inside Stationery. Dogs and Cats inside Animals. |
| Two overlapping inside a big one | Two circles crossing, both within the large circle | Mothers and Sisters inside Women. |
| Three nested | Circle in circle in circle | Delhi inside India inside Asia. |
| Three mutually overlapping | All three cross; a common centre | Teachers, Women, Graduates. |
| One overlapping two separate ones | Middle circle cuts two non-touching circles | Doctors, Men, Women (a doctor can be a man or a woman; no man is a woman). |
| All separate | Three circles apart | Earth, Moon, Mars. |
| Overlap plus separate | Two crossing circles, third apart | Indians, Singers, Tables. |

### Numbers

| Formula | Worked example |
|---|---|
| Neither = Total − (A + B − Both) | 50 students; 30 cricket, 25 football, 10 both → 45 play at least one → **5** neither. |
| Only A = A − Both | 45 speak English, 15 speak both → **30** only English. |
| Exactly one = A + B − 2 × Both | 70 tea, 60 coffee, 30 both → 70 + 60 − 60 = **70**. |
| Three sets: A + B + C − (AB + BC + CA) + ABC | 30 + 25 + 20 − (10 + 8 + 6) + 4 = **55** study at least one. |

## 8. Mathematical operations ⭐

### Sign replacement to balance an equation ⭐

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Find exact divisions among neighbours: 221 ÷ 17 = 13, 69 ÷ 23 = 3, 42 ÷ 21 = 2. The "÷" must sit there. |
| 2 | Note where "=" falls. If the last number is the answer, "=" is the last sign; if two big numbers sit at the ends, "=" may be in the middle. |
| 3 | Test only the surviving options with BODMAS. |

Worked examples:
- 18 * 6 * 4 * 20 * 7 * 25 with "÷, ×, +, −, =" → 18 ÷ 6 × 4 + 20 − 7 = 3 × 4 + 20 − 7 = **25** ✓.
- 19 * 23 * 221 * 17 * 89 * 361 ⭐ → 221 ÷ 17 = 13 is the clue → 19 × 23 + 221 ÷ 17 − 89 = 437 + 13 − 89 = **361** ✓.
- 45 * 45 * 45 * 45 * 45 * 1979 ⭐ → "+, ÷, =, ×, −": 45 + 45 ÷ 45 = 46 and 45 × 45 − 1979 = 2025 − 1979 = 46 ✓ ("=" in the middle).
- 34 * 69 * 23 * 37 * 23 * 882 ⭐ → 34 − 69 ÷ 23 + 37 × 23 = 34 − 3 + 851 = **882** ✓.

### Interchanging two signs ⭐

| Method | Worked example |
|---|---|
| Swap the pair named in each option and compute; stop at the first match | 12 + 6 ÷ 3 − 4 × 2 = 18? Swap + and −: 12 − 6 ÷ 3 + 4 × 2 = 12 − 2 + 8 = **18** ✓. Swapping ÷ and × gives 28. |
| DSSSB check ⭐ | 105 ÷ 21 + 67 − 64 × 16 = 287 → swap × and +: 105 ÷ 21 × 67 − 64 + 16 = 335 − 48 = 287 ✓. |

### Symbol substitution

| Method | Worked example |
|---|---|
| Rewrite the whole expression with the real signs first, then BODMAS | If + means ×, × means −, − means ÷, ÷ means +: 12 + 3 × 5 − 5 ÷ 4 → 12 × 3 − 5 ÷ 5 + 4 = 36 − 1 + 4 = **39**. |
| DSSSB ⭐ | ÷ → ×, − → +, × → ÷, + → −: 38 × 57 ÷ 9 − 18 + 12 → 38 ÷ 57 × 9 + 18 − 12 = 6 + 18 − 12 = **12**. |

### Interchanging two numbers ⭐

| Method | Worked example |
|---|---|
| Swap the two numbers everywhere in each option, then check | Interchange 7 and 9: "9 × 4 − 7 × 3 = 4" becomes 7 × 4 − 9 × 3 = 28 − 27 = 1 ≠ 4 → this equation is **not** correct. "9 − 7 + 3 = 1" becomes 7 − 9 + 3 = 1 ✓. |

### Defined operations

| Method | Worked example |
|---|---|
| Find the rule from the given cases | 2 # 3 = 8, 4 # 5 = 24, 6 # 7 = 48 → a × (b + 1) → 8 # 9 = 8 × 10 = **80**. |
| Letters as signs | P = +, Q = −, R = ×, S = ÷: 20 R 3 P 8 S 4 Q 5 → 60 + 2 − 5 = **57**. |

### Approximation ⭐

| Method | Worked example |
|---|---|
| Round every number to the nearest whole number (or to a friendly multiple), then BODMAS | 56.43 ÷ 27.89 − 67.78 + 98.19 × 13.67 ⭐ → 56 ÷ 28 − 68 + 98 × 14 = 2 − 68 + 1372 = **1306**. |
| Pick the option closest to your value; options are usually 30+ apart | 47.89 ÷ 8.03 × 12.11 − 19.87 → 48 ÷ 8 × 12 − 20 = 72 − 20 = **52**. |

BODMAS order: Brackets → Of → Division and Multiplication (left to right) → Addition and Subtraction (left to right). 24 ÷ 4 × 3 = 18, not 2.

## Exam traps
1. **"All A are B" does not give "All B are A"** ⭐ — only "Some B are A". (All peacocks are eagles ≠ All eagles are peacocks.)
2. **Some + Some = no conclusion** ⭐, even when the words sound related.
3. **Guessed causes** ⭐ (he worked harder, she is ill) never follow from a statement of fact.
4. **"All employees" / "leave the town"** ⭐: blanket punishment and running away are never valid courses of action.
5. **BODMAS left to right** ⭐: 34 − 69 ÷ 23 + 37 × 23 is 34 − 3 + 851, not (34 − 69) ÷ …
6. **Interchange questions**: swap in the whole expression, including any sign that appears twice.
7. **≥ chains**: A ≥ B ≥ C gives only A ≥ C; "A > C" alone does not follow, but "A > C or A = C" is an either–or.
8. **Venn word groups**: Mothers and Sisters overlap (a woman can be both); Pen and Pencil do not.

## Quick self-check
1. All roses are flowers; some flowers fade quickly. Does "Some roses fade quickly" follow? — **No** (All + Some gives nothing).
2. No bat is a bird; all birds are animals. What follows? — **Some animals are not bats**.
3. Statement: "The city metro now runs till 1 a.m." Does "Commuters can now travel by metro later at night" follow? — **Yes** (direct result); "Buses will stop running at night" does **not** (new information).
4. Statement: "Many students fail in mathematics." Course: "Abolish mathematics." Follows? — **No** (extreme; remedial classes would).
5. 15 * 3 * 4 * 8 * 2 * 16 with "÷, ×, −, ÷, =" — check: 15 ÷ 3 × 4 − 8 ÷ 2 = 20 − 4 = **16** ✓.
6. Which two signs must be interchanged to make 10 × 5 + 2 − 6 ÷ 3 = 22 correct? — **+ and ÷**: 10 × 5 ÷ 2 − 6 + 3 = 25 − 6 + 3 = 22.
7. If × means +, + means ÷, ÷ means −, − means ×: 6 × 8 + 2 ÷ 3 − 1 = ? — **7** (6 + 8 ÷ 2 − 3 × 1 = 6 + 4 − 3).
8. A @ B means A > B, A % B means A < B, A & B means A = B. P @ Q, Q & R, R % S. Does "P @ R" follow? — **Yes** (P > Q = R).
9. 100 people: 60 read Hindi papers, 50 English, 20 both. How many read neither? — **10**.
10. Which picture fits Teachers, Women, Graduates? — **Three overlapping circles with a common centre**.
